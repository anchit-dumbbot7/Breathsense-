What is MQ-138?

MQ-138 is a metal-oxide semiconductor gas sensor designed to respond to several gases and volatile compounds. Its broad sensitivity makes it interesting for experimental electronic-nose and breath-VOC research.

Target gases and vapours

The MQ-138 datasheet identifies sensitivity toward several gases and vapours. Exact response depends on concentration, temperature, humidity, calibration and the surrounding gas mixture.

  • Benzene
  • Alcohols
  • Ammonia
  • Smoke
  • n-Hexane
  • VOC mixtures

Why breath analysis?

Human breath contains volatile organic compounds generated by metabolic processes and interactions between the body, lungs and environment. Researchers investigate these compounds as potential biomarkers.

Conditions investigated through breath VOCs

The following conditions have been studied in breath-VOC research. These should be interpreted as potential research associations, not as diseases directly detected by a single MQ-138 sensor.

12 of 12 conditions

  • Diabetes mellitus

    Metabolic

    Altered metabolism can change the concentration of volatile compounds in exhaled breath. Acetone is particularly associated with altered fat metabolism and has been extensively investigated as a breath biomarker.

    Research signal: Acetone / ketones

  • Lung cancer

    Cancer

    Cancer-associated metabolic changes can alter the VOC profile of exhaled breath. Research has explored combinations of hydrocarbons, aldehydes, ketones and other VOCs as potential signatures.

    Research signal: Complex VOC pattern

  • COPD

    Respiratory

    Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress in the respiratory system can modify exhaled VOC patterns. Electronic-nose approaches have been investigated for distinguishing COPD-related breath profiles.

    Research signal: Oxidative-stress VOCs

  • Asthma

    Respiratory

    Airway inflammation can influence the chemical composition of exhaled breath. VOC profiling has therefore been investigated as a non-invasive respiratory monitoring approach.

    Research signal: Inflammation-associated VOC pattern

  • Tuberculosis

    Infection

    Mycobacterial infection and host immune responses can produce changes in volatile compounds. Breath-VOC research has investigated whether these patterns can assist TB screening.

    Research signal: Infection-related VOC profile

  • Liver disease

    Liver and kidney

    The liver plays a central role in metabolism and detoxification. Impaired liver function can therefore influence circulating and exhaled volatile compounds.

    Research signal: Altered metabolic VOCs

  • Kidney disease

    Liver and kidney

    Reduced kidney function can alter the concentration of volatile metabolites, including nitrogen-containing compounds. Breath ammonia has been investigated in relation to renal dysfunction.

    Research signal: Ammonia / nitrogen compounds

  • Gastric cancer

    Cancer

    Changes in gastric metabolism and tumour-associated biochemical processes may produce measurable differences in breath VOC patterns.

    Research signal: Hydrocarbons / aldehydes / ketones

  • Liver cirrhosis

    Liver and kidney

    Advanced liver dysfunction can modify systemic metabolism and consequently change volatile compounds reaching the lungs and exhaled breath.

    Research signal: Altered VOC profile

  • Cystic fibrosis

    Respiratory

    Chronic airway infection and inflammation in cystic fibrosis can produce characteristic changes in exhaled volatile compounds.

    Research signal: Microbial / inflammatory VOCs

  • Pulmonary hypertension

    Respiratory

    Altered pulmonary and vascular metabolism can be reflected in breath chemistry. Researchers have investigated VOC and gas signatures associated with pulmonary vascular disease.

    Research signal: Complex breath-gas pattern

  • Obstructive sleep apnea

    Respiratory

    Repeated changes in respiration, oxygenation and metabolism during sleep may influence exhaled breath composition.

    Research signal: Altered respiratory VOC pattern

How a breath-VOC screening system could work

A scientifically stronger system uses the MQ-138 as one sensing element within a calibrated measurement and classification pipeline.

  1. 01

    Breath collection

    A controlled breath sample is collected using a standardized sampling protocol.

  2. 02

    VOC interaction

    VOCs interact with the sensor's sensing material, changing its electrical resistance.

  3. 03

    Signal processing

    Sensor response is digitized, filtered and converted into features such as response amplitude and recovery.

  4. 04

    Pattern classification

    A validated statistical or machine-learning model can compare the VOC pattern against reference data.

What the sensor cannot claim

Responsible medical technology requires clear boundaries between experimental screening and clinical diagnosis.

  • Not a standalone diagnostic device

    MQ-138 responds to chemical compounds, not to disease names. A disease cannot be inferred from one gas measurement alone.

  • Cross-sensitivity

    Metal-oxide sensors can respond to multiple gases. Food, medication, smoking, environmental pollution and humidity can affect measurements.

  • Sensor drift

    Sensor characteristics can change with time, temperature, humidity and operating history. Calibration is therefore essential.

  • Clinical validation required

    A public medical product requires controlled clinical studies, reference testing, statistical validation, appropriate regulatory review and defined performance metrics.

From a single gas sensor to an electronic nose

The strongest research direction is not to assign a disease to one MQ-138 reading. Instead, an electronic-nose architecture can combine several partially selective sensors and machine-learning algorithms to recognize multivariate VOC patterns.

Scientific foundation

This educational interface is based on published breath-VOC research and MQ-series sensor documentation. Breath-VOC research has investigated respiratory, metabolic, liver, kidney and cancer-related conditions. However, current scientific literature highlights challenges involving selectivity, sensor drift, reproducibility, sampling protocols and clinical validation.

The intended role of this platform is therefore research and screening technology exploration, not replacement of established medical diagnosis.

Common questions

Short answers on what BreathSense is, and what it is not.

Can BreathSense diagnose a disease?

No. MQ-138 responds to chemical compounds, not to disease names, so a disease cannot be inferred from one gas measurement. Diagnosis requires validated medical tests and qualified healthcare professionals.

Why use an MQ-138 sensor at all?

It is a low-cost metal-oxide sensor with broad sensitivity, which makes it a practical starting point for experimental electronic-nose and breath-VOC research.

What can distort a reading?

Food, medication, smoking, environmental pollution and humidity can all affect measurements. Sensor characteristics also drift with time, temperature, humidity and operating history, so calibration is essential.

What would a stronger system look like?

An electronic-nose design that combines several partially selective sensors with machine-learning models, inside a calibrated pipeline: sampling, sensing, signal processing, then pattern classification.

What is needed before this could be a medical product?

Controlled clinical studies, reference testing, statistical validation, appropriate regulatory review and defined performance metrics.
